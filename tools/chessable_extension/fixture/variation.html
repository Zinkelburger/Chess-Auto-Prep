A favorite of Fischer and quite a few top players in the '90s. The problem with this move is that, as engines became stronger, our appreciation for the bishop pair grew. Top players found it attractive to even give up a pawn in the Marshall, just to get the bishop pair! All approved by the engines. For them, bishops are clearly superior pieces, and it takes a strong justification to consider trading them for knights.

Our relationship with engines is a bit like our relationship with Google Maps. At first, we were skeptical and often ignored its suggestions. However, after getting lost a few too many times, when we trusted our instincts instead of the app's suggestions, we started trusting it more and more. Now, very few people think they know better, and those who do are likely to be delusional.

The same happened with chess engines. Back in the '90s or early 2000s, it was still acceptable to dismiss their evaluation when they claimed that taking on c6 was objectively dubious (?!). After all, the line had been trusted by legends like Alekhine, Capablanca, and Fischer. But over time, just like with GPS, we've come to accept that the cold, calculating engine is usually right.

That said, the line still shows up from time to time, which makes it dangerous. If you don't face it often, chances are your theoretical knowledge will have faded − so White may hope to gain a practical edge, at the very least through a time advantage. So, give it some attention.
